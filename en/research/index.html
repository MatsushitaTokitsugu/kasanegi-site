RESEARCH

Searching for Japan's first impact crater with AI

No meteorite impact structure has yet been confirmed in Japan. Between 2025 and 2026, a red relief image map covering all of Japan became available for anyone to view. In this research, we apply processing that we have verified on known impact structures around the world to the whole country, to look for the first one. Our methods and progress are published on this page.

QUESTION

Research question

More than 200 structures formed by meteorite impacts have been confirmed worldwide. In Japan, only Mt. Oikeyama in Nagano Prefecture is regarded as a probable impact structure (Sakamoto et al. 2010). Japan's mountains are steep, and rain and forest change its landforms quickly. Impact structures may therefore have been overlooked.

The Oikeyama structure is a semicircular landform about 900 m across on the south-eastern slope that includes the summit, and its origin has long been debated. Shock-metamorphic features in quartz were reported in 2005. A negative gravity anomaly of about 2 mGal was reported in 2010. The study of Oikeyama also began with its landform.

In recent years, 1 m elevation data from airborne laser scanning have been prepared across Japan. Red relief image maps, which make landforms easy to read, can now be viewed for the whole country. Surveying all of Japan by eye is not realistic. This research therefore combines processing verified on known impact structures with machine-learning classification to examine the whole country.

The only probable structure in Japan

Mt. Oikeyama (Iida, Nagano)

Location
Western foot of the Akaishi Mountains; south-eastern slope including the summit of Mt. Oikeyama (1,905 m)
Form
Semicircular, about 900 m across (about 40% of the circle remains)
Evidence
Shock features in quartz (PDFs); a negative gravity anomaly of about 2 mGal (114 stations)
Status
Probable impact structure
Literature
Sakamoto et al. 2005 (LPSC XXXVI); Sakamoto et al. 2010 (Meteoritics & Planetary Science 45); Sakamoto & Shichi 2010 (Planetary People 19, in Japanese)

Recent open data in Japan

  • 2002Tatsuro Chiba and colleagues devised the red relief image map (Asia Air Survey). It shows slope together with a ridge-valley index, derived from above-ground and below-ground openness, in a single image.
  • 2023The Geospatial Information Authority of Japan (GSI) began providing 1 m elevation data from airborne laser scanning (DEM1A). The patent on the red relief image map expired in the same year.
  • 2025In June, a red relief image map of all of Japan became viewable in the browser (Q-chizu). In October, DEM1A coverage reached about 61% of Japan's tertiary mesh cells.
  • 2026In February, the Forestry Agency began publishing forest elevation data and micro-topography maps from airborne laser scanning. DEM1A coverage also expanded in February and July.

Sources: Chiba et al. 2007 (Chizu 45(1)); Chiba 2026 (Tokyo Geographical Society lecture announcement); GSI announcements (March and October 2025) and DEM1A update history; Q-chizu; Forestry Agency material (March 2026).

Wider applications

The inventor of the red relief image map has noted that it may make landform features easier to recognise for AI as well as for people (Chiba 2026). The methods of this research can also be used in the following fields.

  • OverseasThe same processing and classification can be used in countries and regions where high-resolution elevation data are public.
  • The MoonRed relief image maps have also been made of craters on the far side of the Moon (Chiba 2026, from Kaguya laser altimeter data).
  • ArchaeologyRed relief image maps are used to find burial mounds hidden under forest. In Okuizumo, Shimane, 77 previously unknown keyhole-shaped mounds were reported (San'in Chuo Shimpo, 2023).

METHOD

Research method

The research proceeds in four stages. First, we examine which observations can be used in Japan and how good they are. Second, we apply the same processing to known impact structures around the world, matched to that quality. Third, we build a classifier that does not miss known impact structures. Finally, we apply it to the whole of Japan. All data and processing used are public.

  1. I

    Establish the quality of Japan's observations, place by place

    Data
    GSI digital elevation models (DEM1A to DEM10B), the index of coverage and survey years, public survey records, ground gravity data, aeromagnetic anomalies of Japan
    Work
    We list the observations and processes that can be applied to the whole of Japan. For each, we confirm how it is made, its resolution and its accuracy in the distributor's own documentation. For elevation data, we trace which survey in which year each place comes from.
    So far
    We compiled a list of 90 observation series and 44 process groups. We confirmed that Oikeyama's 1 m elevation comes from an airborne laser survey carried out by Nagano Prefecture in fiscal 2022 for erosion-control planning.
  2. II

    Apply the same processing to known structures worldwide

    Data
    An inventory of 354 impact structures; open 1 m-class elevation data from the US, Canada, Estonia and Germany
    Work
    We make red relief image maps with the same formulas and constants as Q-chizu. Elevation data from abroad are converted to the same tile grid as Japan before processing. One pixel is about 0.98 m at 35°N, and the openness search radius is 50 pixels. Structures are lined up by diameter. They are then measured by distance from the centre.
    So far
    We prepared elevation data and made red relief image maps for 48 structures in four countries. Of these, 43 were processed with the same formulas and constants as Q-chizu. 39 were lined up by diameter and measured.
  3. III

    A classifier that does not miss known structures

    Data
    Outputs of stage II; gravity, magnetic and geological data
    Work
    We test whether known impact structures deliberately left out of training are classified as impact structures without being told their location or type. The pass condition is that no known impact structure is missed. Gravity, magnetic and geological data are combined with the red relief image maps.
    Goal
    To detect even an eroded mountain structure like Oikeyama without prior information. The false-positive rate is not assessed at this stage.
  4. IV

    Apply to the whole of Japan

    Data
    More than a million elevation tiles covering Japan (Q-chizu); nationwide gravity, magnetic and geological data
    Work
    We apply the same processing and classifier to the whole of Japan at once. As the area covered by 1 m elevation data grows, so does the area we can examine.
    Goal
    To choose, from the detected candidates, sites to confirm by geological fieldwork. Evidence of impact includes shocked quartz.

What "the same processing" means

A red relief image map is made by multiplying two images (Chiba et al. 2007). One shows slope as the saturation of red. The other shows the ridge-valley index as brightness. The ridge-valley index is half the difference between above-ground and below-ground openness. It is high on ridges and low in valleys.

We identified the formulas and constants of Q-chizu's computation. Our own processing was matched to them. Elevation data for structures abroad are converted to the same tile grid as Japan before processing. When comparing, we also align the version of the input elevation data, the grid, the search radius and the quality of the original observations, in addition to the formulas and constants.

Source: Chiba, T., Suzuki, Y. and Hiramatsu, T. 2007. Chizu (Map) 45(1): 27–36 (in Japanese).

Checking Japan's public observations

We confirm, from primary sources, the quality of the observations that serve as Japan's reference.

  • MagneticsAeromagnetic data over Japan's land area were acquired in surveys in 1981–1983, with main lines 3–4 km apart, tie lines 20 km apart and a constant altitude of about 1,372 m above sea level. We confirmed this in the original 1985 report.
  • GravityWe used 14,029 GSI ground gravity stations and 173,365 public stations from AIST. We counted the stations around each of 95,484 evaluation points spaced 0.02° apart over land. From the counts, we mapped station density across Japan.
  • 1 m elevationStarting from the GSI index, we traced the source of Oikeyama's 1 m elevation data to the original public survey record. It is an 854 km² airborne laser survey carried out by Nagano Prefecture in fiscal 2022 for erosion-control planning.
  • Source textsWe read the GSI Fundamental Geospatial Data FAQ, the description of the aeromagnetic anomalies of Japan, the JGSN2016 ground gravity data manual and AIST's documentation of the Gravity Database of Japan in the original. The figures were checked against these documents.

CLAUDE

Claude's role

This research is run with Anthropic's Claude (Claude Code) as its supervisor. People set the research questions and make the final decisions. Claude mainly handles the following work.

  • Research designClaude divides the research questions into stages. It designs what is compared at each stage, and how.
  • CheckingEvery figure is checked against the original text of papers, survey records and distributors' documentation. Checked figures are recorded with their sources.
  • Meaning of dataClaude confirms in the original documents what "1 m elevation" is made from and how much of it is measured. It compared the elevation of Q-chizu and GSI DEM1A pixel by pixel (a mean difference of 0.46 m at Oikeyama). From the result, it decided to use Q-chizu's elevation as the input for Japan.
  • Processing checkClaude compared the nationwide map's computation and our own processing on the same elevation data. From the result, it judged that the two match.
  • Briefs and reviewClaude writes briefs for long investigations and implementation work. Results that come back are reviewed against primary sources. Each decision to accept or reject them is recorded.
